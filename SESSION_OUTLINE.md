# sota talk - 45 min outline

28 oct 2026, 8.15 to 9.00am
~200 yr 4 students, school of the arts
renee's topic line: general introduction to synthetic biology - why does it matter?

---

## the premise

we are biological entities living in a biological world.
hence our problems are largely biological problems. food, disease, waste, materials, climate.
yet we solve nearly all of them industrially. heat, pressure, oil, mined rock.

in particular: biology already contains the machinery to do these things.
it evolved. it runs at 37 degrees, in water, out of air & sunlight. it has been refining for ~4 billion yrs.

what was missing was never the machinery. it was the ability to instruct it.
that is synthetic biology.

everything in the talk serves that one line.

---

## the method, repeated 4x

for each problem:

1. the problem
2. how we solve it now
3. what that costs
4. the machinery biology already has
5. what still stands in the way

step 5 is not optional. drop it & we are selling, not teaching.
it is also where the students' research questions come from.

## 1. cold open (2 min)

no introductions. open on their aggregate from the website.
n designs, n classes, most picked village, most picked chassis, 3 strangest things they wanted a cell to detect.

line: "two hundred of you designed an organism last week. we read every single one. we'll come back to what you built. first, why anyone would want to."

they enter as authors, not audience. the forward reference stops this reading as a detour.

---

## 2. the premise (4 min)

3 beats, nothing more.

- every atom in you was put in place at ~37 degrees, normal pressure, out of air water & food. no furnace, no pressure vessel, no acid.
- everything we manufacture is the opposite. heat rock, crack oil, force gases together. steel, cement, fertiliser, plastic, dye.
- hence the mismatch. biological things, biological world, biological problems, industrial solutions.

then: "here's the part that should annoy you. for a lot of these, biology already has the answer. not approximately. better than ours, at room temperature, in water."

careful: not anti-industry. the next example feeds half the planet. the point is mismatch & cost, not villainy.

---

## 3. four problems (13 min, ~3 min each)

### 3.1 feeding people

- problem: plants need nitrogen. air is 78% nitrogen. they can't touch it. N2 bond is one of the strongest in chemistry.
- now: haber-bosch. 450-550 C, 250-350 bar. ~230 Mt ammonia/yr.
- cost: 1-2% of all energy humans use. ~3% of global CO2.
- the line: **~half the nitrogen atoms in your body came out of that process.** you are partly a product of a chemical plant.
- machinery: nitrogenase. bacteria in root nodules of beans & clover. same reaction, in soil, ambient temp & pressure.
- in the way: nitrogenase is killed by oxygen & costs the cell enormous energy. getting it into rice & wheat is a real open problem, decades deep.

this one leads. strongest pair we have.

### 3.2 making materials

- problem: strong fibre, lasting colour.
- now: fibre starts as oil. strongest fibres need high temp & aggressive solvents. dyeing is water & effluent heavy.
- machinery: spider spins silk at room temp out of water, & by weight it absorbs more energy before breaking than steel. bacteria make pigment as ordinary metabolism. mycelium grows into whatever shape you give it.
- in the way: can't farm spiders, they are territorial & eat each other. hence you move the gene into yeast or bacteria & grow it in a tank. **that move is exactly synbio.** several companies attempting it at scale, mixed results.

this gets the most concrete treatment. dye / textile / material is where an arts cohort sees itself.

note: say **tougher** than steel, not stronger. toughness = energy absorbed before breaking. tensile strength is a different claim & we'd be wrong.

### 3.3 finding out what's wrong

- problem: is this water safe. is this person infected. is this crop diseased, early enough to act.
- now: sample -> lab -> expensive instrument -> trained staff -> cold chain -> days.
- cost: that chain doesn't exist in most places where the answer matters most.
- machinery: cells detect specific molecules at vanishing concentration as routine. their nose is doing it right now. bacteria pick single compounds out of a crowded soup.
- the move: put the sensing in a cell, wire it to something visible. paper strip, colour change, no machine, no electricity, no fridge.
- in the way: we have sensors for a small fraction of what we care about. building new ones to order is hard. live frontier.

### 3.4 clearing up

- problem: PET lasts centuries.
- now: landfill, burn, or mechanical recycling that degrades the material each cycle.
- machinery: 2016, bacterium found at a japanese recycling site had evolved an enzyme that cuts PET. enzymes break bonds at ambient that we'd otherwise need heat or harsh chemistry for.
- in the way: wild enzyme far too slow to matter industrially. engineered versions much faster. **that is the actual lesson** - once you can read & rewrite it, you are no longer stuck with whatever evolution left lying around.

### optional 5th, only if early: storing information

data centres draw power continuously, discs last yrs. DNA is absurdly dense & we read it from remains thousands of yrs old. present density as a lab record, not a product.
good for this cohort: ask what they'd archive for a thousand yrs.

## 4. so why isn't everything made this way (4 min)

put the objection to them straight: "if biology is so good at this, why is your jumper still made of oil?"

because for almost all of history we could read biology but not write it. breed, select, wait. couldn't specify.

now we can, & in a way that matters: **in parts that fit together.** promoter, gene, logic.
standard parts are what turned tinkering into engineering. same as standard screw threads.

hence: synthetic biology = the programmability of living systems through genetic circuits.

& it runs as a loop. design, build, test, learn. nobody gets it right first time.

---

## 5. three design reviews (18 min, 6 each)

**this is the point of the session.**

we are not showing their ideas. we are working on them, in front of them, with them, the way we'd work on one of ours at a team meeting.

a 15 yr old told their idea is interesting learns nothing.
a 15 yr old watching 2 people spend 6 min working out what it would actually take learns what research is.

### rules on stage

- no flattery. "great idea" teaches nothing. spending 6 min on it says it better.
- no rescuing. fatal flaw gets named. then ask what it'd take to get round it.
- no performing expertise. when we don't know, say so & say how we'd find out. most useful thing a researcher can model.

### the 6 moves, each review

| | move | time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | read it exactly as written, credited by class. no softening, no laughing | 20s |
| 2 | take it seriously. "if this worked, what changes, & for whom?" | 45s |
| 3 | break it into parts. **ask the room**: what has to be detected, decided, done | 90s |
| 4 | **the first experiment.** "you have a lab & 2 weeks. what do you do monday morning?" | 90s |
| 5 | what kills it. named by category | 60s |
| 6 | the research question. one sentence, on screen | 30s |

**review 1 only**, add one line at move 3:
"notice what we just did. we turned a sentence into a sensor, a rule & an output. that's a genetic circuit. you wrote one last week without being told the name."

that is the whole reveal. 10 seconds, because it lands inside real work instead of as a lecture.

**move 4 is the most valuable 90 seconds of the session.** take answers from the floor, then give the real one: smallest cheapest test that could tell you you're wrong. name the equipment. name what gets measured. make it sound like something a person could actually do, because it is.

### where the hands-on sits

renee asked for a small hands-on. it lives inside the reviews, not beside them. students do 3 things each time:

- supply the parts at move 3, called out from the floor
- 30s turn to your neighbour at move 4: "what would you test first?" -> take 2 answers on the mic
- call the failure at move 5 before we do

by review 3 they should be running moves 3 & 5 nearly unaided. if they are, it worked.

### choosing the 3

reviews are only as good as the choice. one of each:

- **nearly buildable** -> ends somewhere hopeful. first experiment a school could almost run.
- **wildly ambitious but real mechanism** -> shows how a huge idea gets cut to a tractable first step without being dismissed.
- **raises an ethical question** (containment, or who benefits) -> ethics arrives through their own work, not as a lecture block. room argues it out.

order: achievable, then impossible-made-tractable, then consequential.

**each one needs ~1 hr of real prep.** not slides. actually find out: does the sensor exist, what's the nearest real part, what's the first experiment, what's the honest killer.
**if we can't write a first experiment for a design, it's the wrong design.**

---

## 6. research question + handover (4 min)

3 questions now on the wall, all from ideas written in this room. use them.

1. narrow enough to attack. not "how do we fix plastic" but "can this enzyme break down PET at seawater temperature"
2. built on something measurable. name the thing that changes & how you'd know
3. **falsifiable.** you can say what would prove you wrong. almost nobody teaches this. most useful habit in science.

spend the most time on 3.

close: "we did that 3 times this morning, on ideas written by people sitting in this room, & every one ended in a question somebody could go & answer. you've all got one. yours is the part you couldn't work out how to do."

hand to renee. nothing after.

## cut order if behind

1. problem 3.4 (clearing up)
2. the optional 5th problem
3. review 3 down to 4 min by dropping moves 2 & 5
4. the neighbour turn in review 2

**never cut:** the premise, 2 of the 4 problems, section 4, 2 full reviews, move 4 in any review that runs, or section 6.

2 problems + 2 complete reviews beats 4 rushed problems + 3 thin reviews.
