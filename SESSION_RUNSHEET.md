## The argument

Renee's topic sentence is **why does it matter**, so the session has to earn that answer rather than assert it. The spine:

> **We are biological entities living in a biological world.** Our largest problems are biological problems: food, disease, waste, materials, climate. Yet we solve almost all of them industrially, with heat, pressure, solvents and mined rock.
>
> **Biology already solved many of them**, at room temperature, in water, out of air and sunlight. **The machinery exists.** It has been running and refining for about four billion years.
>
> The only thing missing, until recently, was the ability to **instruct** that machinery. That is synthetic biology.

Every section serves that. The method, repeated four times, is:

**A real problem → how we solve it today → what that costs → the biological machinery that already does it → what still stands in the way.**

That last step matters. If we only show the elegant biological answer we are selling, not teaching.

## The one thing this session has to do

Their assignment over the next two days is to **propose research questions**. So the test is:

> Do 200 arts students walk out with better research questions than they would have had without us?

**The second half is three student ideas, developed seriously.** Not shown, not sorted, not used as examples. Developed, the way we would develop one of our own: broken into parts, costed against what exists, given a first experiment, and attacked honestly. The room does the developing with us.

This is the whole point of the session. A fifteen year old being told their idea is interesting learns nothing. A fifteen year old watching a team of people spend six minutes working out what it would actually take learns what research is.

## 1. Cold open, 2 min

**Do not open with "hello, we are from NUS."** Open on the screen showing their aggregate: how many designs came in, how many classes, the most chosen problem area and organism, and the three strangest things somebody wanted a cell to detect.

> "Two hundred of you designed an organism last week. We read every single one. We will come back to what you built. First, why anyone would want to."

**Why:** they enter as authors, not audience, and the hook forward stops this feeling like a detour.

---

## 2. The premise, 4 min

The whole talk in one move. Three beats:

**You were assembled at body temperature.** Every atom in you was put in place at about 37 degrees, at normal pressure, out of air, water and food. No furnace. No high pressure vessel. No concentrated acid.

**Everything we manufacture is the opposite.** We make things by heating rock, cracking oil, and pushing gases together hard enough to force a reaction. Steel, cement, fertiliser, plastic, dye.

**So there is a mismatch.** We are biological things, living in a biological world, with biological problems, and we solve almost all of them industrially.

> "Here is the part that should annoy you. For a lot of these problems, biology already has the answer. Not approximately. Better than ours, at room temperature, in water."

**Trap:** do not make this anti-industry or anti-chemistry. The Haber-Bosch example that follows feeds half the planet. The point is mismatch and cost, not villainy.

---

## 3. Four problems, 14 min

Three and a half minutes each. Same five-step shape every time, so the pattern itself teaches.

### 3.1 Feeding people

- **Problem.** Plants need nitrogen. The air is 78% nitrogen. They cannot touch it, because N₂ is held together by one of the strongest bonds in chemistry.
- **How we do it now.** The Haber-Bosch process. **450 to 550 °C, 250 to 350 bar.** About **230 million tonnes of ammonia a year**.
- **What it costs.** **1 to 2% of all energy humans use**, and about **3% of global CO₂ emissions**.
- **The line to land.** Roughly **half the nitrogen atoms in your body** came out of that process. You are, in part, a product of a chemical plant.
- **The machinery that already exists.** Bacteria in the root nodules of beans and clover do the same reaction with an enzyme called **nitrogenase**, in soil, at ambient temperature and pressure.
- **What still stands in the way.** Nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen and costs the cell an enormous amount of energy to run. Moving it into cereals like rice and wheat is a genuine open problem that serious groups have worked on for decades.

### 3.2 Making materials

- **Problem.** We want fibres that are strong, and colour that stays.
- **How we do it now.** Most synthetic fibre starts as oil. High strength fibres need high temperature and aggressive solvents. Dyeing is water and effluent heavy.
- **The machinery that already exists.** A spider spins silk at room temperature, out of water, and by weight it is tougher than steel. Bacteria make vivid pigments as ordinary metabolites. Fungal mycelium grows into whatever shape you give it.
- **What still stands in the way.** You cannot farm spiders, they are territorial and eat each other. So you move the silk gene into yeast or bacteria and grow it in a tank. That move is exactly synthetic biology, and several companies are attempting it at real scale with mixed results.
- **For this room.** This is the mode an arts cohort can see themselves in. Dye, textile, pigment, material.

### 3.3 Finding out what is wrong

- **Problem.** Is this water safe. Is this person infected. Is this crop diseased, early enough to act.
- **How we do it now.** Take a sample, send it to a laboratory, run it on expensive instruments, with trained staff, a cold chain and a wait of days.
- **The machinery that already exists.** Cells detect specific molecules at vanishing concentrations as a matter of routine. Your nose does it right now. Bacteria sense and respond to single compounds in a crowded soup.
- **The move.** Put that sensing into a cell and wire it to something you can see. A paper strip that changes colour, needing no machine, no electricity and no fridge.
- **What still stands in the way.** A sensor exists for only a small fraction of the molecules we care about. Building new ones to order is hard, and is one of the field's live frontiers.

### 3.4 Clearing up

- **Problem.** PET plastic lasts for centuries.
- **How we do it now.** Landfill, incineration, or mechanical recycling that degrades the material each cycle.
- **The machinery that already exists.** In 2016 a bacterium found at a Japanese recycling site turned out to have evolved an enzyme that cuts PET apart. Enzymes break bonds at ambient temperature that we would otherwise need heat or harsh chemistry for.
- **What still stands in the way.** The wild enzyme is far too slow to matter industrially. Engineered versions are much faster, which is itself the point: once you can read and rewrite it, you can improve on what evolution happened to leave you.

**Optional fifth, if running early: storing information.** Data centres consume power continuously and their media last years. DNA is dense enough that a gram could in principle hold hundreds of exabytes, and we routinely read DNA from remains tens of thousands of years old. Good for an arts cohort: ask what they would choose to archive for a thousand years.

## 4. So why isn't everything made this way? 4 min

> "If biology is so good at this, why is your jumper still made of oil?"

**Because for almost all of history we could read biology but not write it.** We could breed, select and wait. We could not specify.

Now we can, and in a particular way that matters: **in parts that fit together**. A promoter, a gene, a piece of logic. Standard parts are what turned tinkering into engineering, the same way standard screw threads did.

**So:** synthetic biology is the **programmability of living systems** through genetic circuits.

And it runs as a loop: **design, build, test, learn.** Nobody gets it right first time. They are about to do one turn of that loop.

---

## 5. Three design reviews, 6 min each

### The stance

We are not showing their ideas. We are **working on them**, in front of them, with them, the way we would work on one of our own at a team meeting. The students are the authors; we are the people they brought it to.

Three rules for the people on stage:

- **No flattery.** "Great idea" teaches nothing. Spending six minutes on it says it better.
- **No rescuing.** If a design has a fatal problem, we find it together and say so. Then we ask what it would take to get round it.
- **No performance of expertise.** When we do not know, we say we do not know, and say how we would find out. That is the most useful thing a researcher can model.

### The six moves, each review

**1. Read it as written (20 s).** Verbatim, credited by class. No editing, no softening, no laughing.

**2. Take it seriously (45 s).** "If this worked, what would actually change, and for whom?" Name the real stake. This is where the room learns their classmate's idea was not a joke.

**3. Break it into parts (90 s).** Ask the room, do not tell them. What has to be *detected*? What has to be *decided*? What has to *happen*? Then: which of those does biology already do, and which does not exist yet?

> In **review 1 only**, add one line here: "Notice what we just did. We turned a sentence into a sensor, a rule and an output. That is a genetic circuit. You wrote one last week without being told the name."

That is the entire reveal, and it costs ten seconds because it arrives inside real work instead of as a lecture.

**4. The first experiment (90 s).** The most valuable ninety seconds of the session.

> "You have a lab and two weeks. Not ten years. What do you do on Monday morning?"

Take answers from the room, then give the real one: the **smallest, cheapest test that could tell you that you are wrong.** Name the equipment. Name what you would measure. Make it sound like something a person could actually do, because it is.

**5. What kills it (60 s).** Honest, and named by category: we cannot sense that yet, the cell cannot afford it, we cannot contain it, the energy is not there, thermodynamics says no.

**6. The research question (30 s).** One sentence, on screen, written out. Their idea now has the shape of real work.

### Where the audience does the work

Renee asked for a hands on element. It lives inside the reviews rather than beside them, and the students do three things in each one:

- Supply the parts at move 3, called out from the floor
- **Thirty seconds, turn to your neighbour, at move 4:** "what would you test first?" Then take two answers on the roving mic
- Call the failure at move 5 before we do

By review 3 they should be running moves 3 and 5 almost unaided. If they are, the session has done its job.

### Choosing the three

This is most of the preparation, and the reviews are only as good as the choice. Pick one of each:

| | What it does for the room |
|---|---|
| **One nearly buildable** | Ends somewhere hopeful. Shows a real first experiment that a school could almost run |
| **One wildly ambitious with a real mechanism** | Shows how an enormous idea gets cut down to a tractable first step without being dismissed |
| **One that raises an ethical question** | Containment, or who benefits. The ethics arrive through their own work rather than as a lecture block, and the room argues it out |

Put the ambitious one second, so the sequence runs achievable, impossible-made-tractable, then consequential.

**Each review needs real preparation**, not slides. Before the day, actually do the work: find whether the sensor exists, find the nearest real part, work out the first experiment, and find the honest killer. Roughly an hour per design. That hour is the session.

## 6. What makes a research question, and handoff, 4 min

Three criteria, fast, pointing back at the three questions now on the wall:

1. **Narrow enough to attack.** Not "how do we fix plastic" but "can this enzyme break down PET at seawater temperature".
2. **Built on something measurable.** Name the thing that changes and how you would know.
3. **Falsifiable.** You can say what would prove you wrong. Almost nobody teaches this and it is the most useful habit in science.

Then:

> "We did that three times this morning, on ideas written by people sitting in this room, and every one of them ended in a question somebody could actually go and answer. You have all got one. Yours is the part you could not work out how to do."

Hand to Ms Renee.

## If running behind

Cut in this order:

1. Problem 3.4, clearing up, 3 min
2. The optional fifth problem, if it was in
3. **Design review 3 down to four minutes** by dropping moves 2 and 5
4. The neighbour turn in review 2

**Never cut:** the premise, at least two of the four problems, section 4, **two full design reviews**, move 4 in any review that runs, or section 6.

A session that covers two problems and two complete reviews is better than one that rushes four problems and three thin reviews.
